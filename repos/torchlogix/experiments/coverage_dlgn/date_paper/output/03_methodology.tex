\section{LogicConnect Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

LogicConnect uses the input representation and layer dimensions to construct connections before training, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:overview}. Semantic pairing exploits known relationships between encoded image values, while multiscale matching extends the construction to hidden signals whose spatial meaning is no longer explicit. Both components produce fixed predecessor indices, allowing training with the original gate parameterization.



\subsection{Network representation}

For a layer $l$ containing $m_l$ two-input gates and $n_l$ available predecessors, we represent connectivity by $I_l\in\{0,\ldots,n_l-1\}^{2\times m_l}$. Gate $j$ receives distinct predecessors $a=I_l[0,j]$ and $b=I_l[1,j]$. Following the baseline DLGN formulation \cite{petersen2022}, its relaxed output is

\begin{equation}
h_{l,j}=\sum_{q=0}^{15}\mathrm{softmax}(\theta_{l,j})_q\,\widetilde f_q(h_{l-1,a},h_{l-1,b}).
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

Here, $\theta_{l,j}$ contains the trainable gate logits and $\widetilde f_q$ denotes the continuous relaxation of Boolean function $q$. Training optimizes $\theta$ on the fixed graph, and discretization selects the highest-weight function for each gate. LogicConnect determines $I_l$ while preserving this optimization, the loss function, and output aggregation.

We associate each predecessor $i$ with a structural ancestry set $A_i$ describing its accessible sources. At a dense image input, labels identify raw channel-pixel values, with thermometer thresholds of the same value sharing a label. Propagating $A_j=A_a\cup A_b$ records sources that can reach each gate through its connections. The learned gate function determines which accessible inputs actually affect the output.

\subsection{Semantic input pairing}

The image encoding provides useful information that is lost when inputs are treated only as numbered bits. In particular, thresholds of the same pixel-channel value share a source, whereas bits from different positions or channels can convey distinct image information. We use this distinction to construct candidate pairs from channel, spatial, and threshold metadata, excluding pairs whose two bits come from the same raw source.

Spatial candidates connect horizontal and vertical positions at power-of-two strides up to half the corresponding axis length, using cyclic boundaries. Additional candidates at the finest scale connect aligned channels and combine different thresholds with a spatial displacement. The construction cycles through these candidate stages, distributing connections across the available relationships while controlling how often each predecessor is selected.

Within each stage, candidates are ordered by the sum of endpoint fan-outs, then their maximum fan-out, and finally their indices. The initial selection examines up to 64 unused candidates in a seeded affine order; once distinct pairs are exhausted, repeated pairs follow the same degree priority. This bounded search preserves a deterministic construction while limiting the cost of selecting semantically defined pairs.

\subsection{Multiscale matching in hidden layers}

Deeper signals lack a direct image-coordinate interpretation, so we construct their connections through regular matchings over predecessor indices and use ancestry to distinguish their accessible sources. Each matching contains disjoint pairs, allowing a predecessor to participate at most once per stage. Varying the matching changes which signals are combined while controlling predecessor reuse.

For $n$ predecessors, the construction considers $K=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ scales. When $n$ is a power of two, scale $s$ pairs index $i$ with $i\mathbin{\oplus}2^s$, providing connections at different index separations. For other widths, an affine traversal with a coprime step pairs adjacent elements; at odd widths, the unmatched predecessor rotates. Each complete stage contains $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ pairs. If fewer gates remain, a partial stage selects disjoint pairs by endpoint degree sum, maximum degree, and index.

We select stages by prioritizing balanced reuse and then considering the overlap between their ancestry sets. Let $d_i$ be the current fan-out of predecessor $i$, and let $\delta_i(P_s)$ count its occurrences in the pairs retained from candidate stage $s$. The resulting degree spread is

\begin{equation}
D_s=\max_i(d_i+\delta_i(P_s))-\min_i(d_i+\delta_i(P_s)).
\label{eq:degree}
\end{equation}

Minimizing $D_s$ gives preference to stages that maintain uniform access to preceding signals. Among candidates with the same spread, we favor pairs with less relative ancestry overlap, measured by

\begin{equation}
N_s=\frac{1}{|Q_s|}\sum_{(a,b)\in Q_s}\left(1-\frac{|A_a\cap A_b|}{\max(1,\min(|A_a|,|A_b|))}\right).
\label{eq:novelty}
\end{equation}

The set $Q_s$ contains the complete candidate matching, or 2,048 evenly indexed pairs when the matching is larger. We use the same set when selecting a partial stage. Identical nonempty ancestry sets contribute zero to this score, while disjoint sets contribute one. LogicConnect minimizes $D_s$ and then maximizes $N_s$, resolving remaining ties by cyclic nominal scale order. Every scale is used once per cycle, as summarized in Algorithm \ref{alg:construction}. Ancestry overlap is a structural heuristic whose contribution to accuracy is evaluated separately.

For even widths, complete matchings increment every degree equally, and a final partial matching increments each degree at most once. Starting from uniform degrees therefore gives a final spread of at most one. For example, eight predecessors feeding ten gates use two complete matchings and two additional pairs, producing degrees of two or three. Degree-preserving ablations test the accuracy contribution of pair identities beyond this balance property.

\subsection{Extension to convolutional networks}

Convolutional DLGNs share logic trees across spatial positions, requiring the construction to preserve both channel organization and spatial sampling \cite{petersen2024}. We apply LogicConnect to channel pairing while retaining the original receptive-field sampler, depth-three logic trees, weight sharing, and OR pooling. The first channel assignment uses semantic metadata, and subsequent channel groups use multiscale matching. This formulation changes connectivity at the channel level without replacing the convolutional computation with independent dense gates.

Channel ancestry initially uses encoded-channel identities. We add an identity label to each output channel so that channels remain distinguishable when their upstream support sets become identical. After flattening, classifier inputs retain this ancestry and receive spatial-feature identities; semantic pairing constructs the classifier input connections, followed by deeper matching stages. These labels support a common construction at different signal granularities, rather than representing exact pixel-dependence sets.

\subsection{Construction overhead}

Construction uses packed ancestry bitsets; CPU storage grows with layer width and label universe. Each stage decision considers $K$ matchings and evaluates at most 2,048 ancestry pairs per candidate. We measure this one-time cost separately from training. A dense layer retains $16m_l$ trainable gate logits and $2m_l$ fixed indices, discarding ancestry before deployment. The circuit retains its logical budget; physical area and interconnect cost depend on synthesis and placement.
